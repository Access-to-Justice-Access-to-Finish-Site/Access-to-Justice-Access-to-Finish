The Story

When Access to Justice Becomes a Maze

This is a story about trying to use the systems that are supposed to resolve problems—and discovering that access to a process is not always the same thing as access to an outcome.

I came to law school to finish something larger than a degree: to build a stable future in a profession I had worked toward for years. What followed was not one isolated dispute. It became a sequence of overlapping academic, administrative, human-rights and legal processes, each with its own language, timelines and gatekeepers.

I kept engaging. I documented meetings, correspondence, decisions and attempts to find a workable route forward. I sought internal help. I used formal complaint processes. I pursued external routes. I asked for solutions that would let me continue my education while protecting my health, safety and professional future.

The central problem became circular: the more routes I used, the harder it became to identify who could make the present academic decision that actually mattered.

The point of this campaign

This campaign is not asking the public to adjudicate every disputed fact. It is not asking people to reduce a complicated institutional history to heroes and villains. It is asking a simpler present-tense question: after years of process, what is the fair and authorized pathway for me to finish my JD?

That distinction matters. Accountability and compassion can coexist. A person can document institutional harm without claiming that every individual involved acted with the same intent. A university can have many departments and procedures and still leave a student without a usable answer. A legal process can exist and still move too slowly to solve an academic deadline.

Why I am telling the story now

The Fall 2026 term begins September 9, 2026. My immediate objective is practical: a written academic determination, appropriate accommodations and boundaries, and a plan to complete my remaining degree requirements.

I have spent years trying to resolve this through formal channels. Public communication is not a substitute for those processes. It is a way to make the record understandable, preserve the chronology, and invite constructive support for a fair path forward.

What finishing means

Finishing is not about winning a public argument. It means completing the education I began, preserving the professional future I worked for, and moving into the next chapter of my life with a structure that is safe, stable and academically workable.
